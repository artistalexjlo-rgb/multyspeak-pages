Traditional Banks: how it works in Brazil — first-hand

From real experience

Opening bank accounts and obtaining credit/debit cards in Brazil as a non-resident can be challenging.
Many banks require a Cadastro de Pessoas Físicas (CPF) and proof of residency, or an existing Visto Temporário (temporary visa). Some banks, like Nubank, may have specific requirements or deny applications based on credit history or lack of local ID. Options for non-residents may include: 1. **Sicredi:** Reports suggest it might open accounts with just a CPF. 2. **Santander:** Some individuals have successfully opened accounts. 3. **Bradesco:** May offer options, but specific requirements need verification. 4. **Banco Rendimento:** Website navigation can be difficult; CPF might be a prerequisite. For international usability, it's important to check if the card supports international transactions and if the bank has specific protocols for travel notifications. SMS verification is often linked to a local phone number, which can be an issue with roaming.

Opening a Brazilian bank account without permanent residency (RNE/CRNM) is difficult.
Traditional banks (Banco do Brasil, Itaú) are highly restrictive for tourists. Fintech options like Nubank or PicPay are more accessible, though some (C6 Bank) charge for account maintenance and debit card issuance. International transfers via SWIFT to Brazilian accounts are often slow, expensive (due to exchange rate spreads and bank fees), and prone to compliance blocks. Alternative methods like P2P or regulated crypto-to-fiat gateways are often preferred for higher volumes.

Opening a traditional Brazilian bank account typically requires a permanent residency permit (VNJ) in addition to CPF.
While some digital neo-banks or crypto card services might theoretically allow account opening with just CPF, success rates have decreased recently, and additional documentation may still be requested. For most foreigners, VNJ is a prerequisite for a standard bank account.

When opening a Banco do Brasil account, physical debit card delivery can take up to a month, despite initial estimates of 10 days.
The virtual card may not appear immediately in the mobile application. International online payments (e.g., Airbnb, Facebook, Telegram Premium) often do not work with Brazilian debit cards, requiring a credit card or a credit limit. Some fintechs like PicPay or Neon offer credit limits secured by a deposit. Obtaining a credit card from traditional banks like Banco do Brasil might require an established banking history (e.g., several months). It is reported that bank branch policies regarding card issuance and in-person collection can vary significantly.

Opening a bank account in Brazil without residency is extremely difficult.
Online banks like Wise and Revolut require a physical RNM (residence card) and will not open accounts with just a CPF. Non-residents/tourists can try mobile wallets like RecargaPay or InfinitePay, though approval is inconsistent. For those with a VNJ Protocol (temporary paper slip before the physical RNM card), major banks like Caixa, Bradesco, Nubank, and C6 usually refuse service. The only bank with a realistic chance of accepting a paper protocol is Banco do Brasil, though you may need to try multiple branches.

Opening a bank account in Brazil as a foreigner, even without a permanent resident visa (Visto Permanente), is often possible.
Visiting a bank branch with funds to deposit or stating that you are expecting a transfer can facilitate the process. Banks like Bradesco may be open to opening accounts under these circumstances. While some banks offer rapid online applications (e.g., Itaú), others might require an in-person visit. Services like Infinity Pay are sometimes discouraged due to potential account blocking issues.

Opening a bank account in Brazil without a Residence Permit (VNE) is not guaranteed and often depends on individual circumstances and bank policies.
Banco do Brasil is a commonly suggested traditional bank to attempt. Caixa Econômica Federal offers the possibility of opening an account via its mobile application, requiring only a CPF and a local phone number. Crucially, the biometric verification process within the app must be completed accurately (e.g., good lighting, correct camera positioning) to avoid being redirected to a physical branch.

To open a bank account in Brazil, one typically needs a residency permit (VNJ).
With the VNJ protocol and proof of address, one can visit traditional banks like Banco do Brasil. Neobanks like NuBank may offer a simpler process. Banco do Brasil is often considered the main Brazilian state-owned bank. Wise is recommended for international transfers. Crédito consignado (deducted from salary) is a type of loan often associated with government employees but can be offered by various banks.

It is possible to open a bank account with Bradesco in Brazil using only a phone number and a CPF (Cadastro de Pessoas Físicas - Brazilian individual taxpayer registry number).
Initially, a debit card for Pix transactions is issued. Subsequently, a multi-currency debit Visa card can be obtained via the Bradesco mobile application. However, success rates vary; some users report being denied. Visiting a bank branch in person with a reference (e.g., photo of a successfully opened card) might improve the chances.

Opening a traditional Brazilian bank account (Banco do Brasil, Itaú, Caixa) as a tourist is extremely difficult as they typically require permanent residency (RNE/RNM) and proof of address.
Tourists needing local payment solutions (PIX) are advised to look into crypto-platforms that support PIX, such as ByBit or digital wallets like WanderWallet, as traditional neo-banks (Nubank, C6) also mandate local plastic ID cards.

Foreigners holding only a provisional refugee protocol (protocolo de solicitação de refúgio) can open physical bank accounts in Brazil.
Banco do Brasil is the most reliable institution for this. To open the account, the applicant must present the valid paper protocol and a proof of address (comprovante de residência). Digital banks like Nubank or traditional ones like Santander generally require the physical RNM/RNE card and will reject the paper protocol.

Opening a bank account in Brazil with just a temporary RNM protocol (rather than the physical plastic card) is highly restricted.
Neobanks like Nubank and Revolut strictly require the physical RNM card. Traditional banks like Itaú often reject applications automatically at the system level despite manual manager initiation. Banco do Brasil (BB) remains the most reliable option for protocol holders, though card issuance takes about 2 weeks.

Foreigners without Brazilian residency (VNJ) may struggle to open a bank account.
An Argentinian DNI is generally not sufficient. However, some individuals have successfully opened accounts at Banco do Brasil by providing proof of residence and explaining their long-term stay and ongoing residency permit application (protocolo de VNJ).

Some branches can override this via manual verification by contacting the central office. The process takes approximately 1.5 hours.
